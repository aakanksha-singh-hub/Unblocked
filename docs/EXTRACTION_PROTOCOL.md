# Reply-understanding study: protocol

This is the only part of the project that produces evidence about the world
rather than about our own generator. Everything else measures policy quality
inside assumptions we wrote. This measures whether a model can read what an
Indian AP clerk actually types.

It is therefore worth more than another thousand simulated buyers, and it is
also the part with a human dependency, so it starts first.

## Design commitments, fixed before any data is collected

1. **We do not write the replies.** Contributors are given a situation and write
   what they would send. They are never shown our intent taxonomy, our label
   names, or any example reply.
2. **We do not label the replies.** Two annotators who are not the project author
   label independently from a written codebook.
3. **Agreement is reported before accuracy.** Cohen's kappa goes on the slide
   above the model number. If two humans cannot agree what a message means,
   model accuracy on those items is not measuring comprehension.
4. **The test split is opened once.** A development split is drawn first and used
   for all prompt iteration. The test split is scored once, at the end, and the
   number reported is that number - not the best of several.
5. **Disagreements are kept.** Items the annotators disputed are adjudicated,
   marked `hard`, and reported as a separate subset. Dropping them would inflate
   the headline by discarding exactly the cases that matter.
6. **No simulator text enters this corpus.** Enforced by
   `tests/test_no_template_leak.py`.

## Target

150-200 items. Small, and we say so: the reported figure carries a Wilson
interval, and at n=180 that interval is roughly +/-7pp. We report the interval,
not the point estimate.

Stratification target, chosen so rare-but-costly intents are not swamped:

| Intent | Target | Why |
|---|---:|---|
| `promise_to_pay` | 40 | The stopping-rule trigger. Most consequential to get right. |
| `process_deflection` | 30 | The hardest class - true for process-bound buyers, a brush-off from avoiders. |
| `dispute` | 30 | Blocks payment entirely; needs an amount extracted too. |
| `payment_claim` | 25 | Must be reconciled against the ledger, never believed. |
| `document_request` | 20 | High-yield and easy to miss. |
| `hardship` | 20 | Mislabelling this as refusal is the error that does human damage. |
| `acknowledgement` | 20 | The contentless class. A model that reads "noted" as a promise is dangerous. |
| `refusal` | 10 | Rare. |

## Elicitation

Contributors get one scenario at a time, in this form, with no taxonomy:

> You handle payments at a company. A supplier has messaged you about an unpaid
> bill. Reply the way you actually would - WhatsApp, email, however you'd really
> send it. Hinglish, short forms, typos, whatever is natural. Don't write it
> formally for our benefit.
>
> **Situation:** {scenario}
>
> Your reply:

Scenarios are generated by `scripts/elicit.py` and deliberately describe
*circumstances*, never intents. "You intend to pay after your GST filing clears"
is a circumstance. "Make a promise to pay" would be leaking the label.

Ask contributors for a spread of registers - some will write formal email
English, some pure WhatsApp Hinglish. Both are real. Do not filter for the ones
that look hard.

### Who to ask

People who work in or around small businesses: family businesses, AP or accounts
roles, anyone who has been on either end of a payment chase. Twelve to fifteen
contributors writing ten to fifteen replies each gets you there.

### What to tell them

That it is for a student project on payment reminders, that replies will be
published in an open dataset, and that they should not include real company
names, real amounts, real people or anything from an actual conversation. The
consent line is in `scripts/elicit.py` and goes at the top of every batch.

## Annotation

Two annotators, working independently, from `docs/CODEBOOK.md`. They see the
scenario and the reply, and assign:

- one intent from the taxonomy
- a promised date where one is expressed, plus the raw text it came from
- a disputed amount where one is expressed
- a claimed UTR/reference where one is present
- a confidence flag: `clear` or `ambiguous`

Annotators must not discuss items before both passes are complete. Adjudication
happens afterwards, on disagreements only, with both annotators present.

Cohen's kappa is computed on intent. Below 0.6, the finding is that the taxonomy
is underspecified, and that is what we report - it would be a real result about
the problem, not a failure to hide.

## Scoring

Reported in this order:

1. Inter-annotator kappa, overall and per intent.
2. Majority-class baseline. Any model figure is quoted against it.
3. Model macro-F1 with Wilson intervals, on the adjudicated test split.
4. The same, on the `hard` subset alone.
5. Abstention rate and abstention precision - a model that says "I am not sure"
   on genuinely ambiguous items is behaving correctly, and this is the one place
   the system is rewarded for declining to answer.
6. Confusion matrix, with `hardship -> refusal` called out. Reading someone who
   cannot pay as someone who will not is how automated collections hurts people.

## What would make us drop this from the submission

Kappa below 0.4, or model macro-F1 within the Wilson interval of the
majority-class baseline. In either case the reply-understanding layer is
unsupported and the video says so rather than quietly cutting the slide.
